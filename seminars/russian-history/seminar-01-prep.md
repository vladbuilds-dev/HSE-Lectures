## ВОПРОС 1
**How is historical knowledge produced? What are historical sources (primary and secondary)? Provide some examples and try to elaborate on the features of working with these sources.**

### What to say (EN)

**Definitions.** Primary sources are original materials produced during the period under study, by people who witnessed or took part in the events. Secondary sources are later works — books, articles, textbooks — that analyse, evaluate and synthesise information taken from primary sources.

**Examples of primary sources** (UCLA groups them in three families):
- *Original documents*: letters, diaries, manuscripts, official documents, treaties, maps, original film footage — e.g. the original text of the U.S. Constitution, a traveller's diary, a firsthand description of a battle.
- *Relics and artefacts*: arrowheads, pottery shards, remains of buildings, clothing, statues.
- *Literature and art*: Homer's Iliad and Odyssey, court poetry, music, drawings, cave paintings.

**Examples of secondary sources**: monographs and journal articles, textbooks, encyclopaedias and dictionaries, bibliographies, most websites.

**Crucial point — the distinction is relational, not absolute.** A 1950s Soviet textbook is a secondary source if I study the reign of Ivan IV, but a primary source if I study Soviet ideology and how the USSR wrote its own history. The status of a source depends on the research question, not on the object itself.

**How historical knowledge is actually produced** — four steps:
1. *Heuristics* — locating and gathering sources (archives, libraries, digital repositories).
2. *External criticism* — is the source authentic? Who wrote it, when, where? Is the copy reliable? (dating, provenance, forgery detection, palaeography, diplomatics)
3. *Internal criticism* — what does it mean? What was the author's purpose, who was the intended audience, what could the author actually know, what are their interests and biases?
4. *Interpretation and synthesis* — combining sources into an argument, publishing it, exposing it to peer review and to criticism by other historians.

**Features of working with sources.** UCLA stresses reading critically: always ask who created the document, why, for whom, and how reliable it is; and judge the source in its own historical context rather than by present-day standards. Two more practical features: sources survive unevenly (what reaches us is already a biased sample — elites and institutions produce and preserve more records than peasants), and a single source is almost never enough: historians triangulate, cross-checking independent sources against each other.

## ВОПРОС 2
**Can historical sources be perceived as "raw data"? What are the differences of working with unstructured historical data (as memoirs) compared to structured datasets (as statistical tables)?**

### What to say (EN)

**Short answer: no.** No historical source is "raw". Every source was produced by someone, for a purpose, inside an institution, and it survived for reasons that are themselves historical. What looks like raw material is already the product of selection — by the author, by the archive, and by centuries of loss. Data in history are never simply "given"; they are *taken* — the researcher decides what counts as evidence and how to categorise it.

**Unstructured sources (memoirs, letters, chronicles):**
- Retrospective and self-justifying: memoirs are written after the fact, often to defend a reputation or shape a legacy. Memory is reconstructive, not a recording.
- No fixed schema — meaning is carried by narrative, rhetoric, silences, and what is *not* said.
- Method: close reading, hermeneutics, contextualisation. Small n, deep interpretation.
- Strength: they give motivation, experience and subjectivity — exactly what tables cannot give.

**Structured sources (censuses, tax rolls, parish registers, price series, trade statistics):**
- They allow aggregation, comparison over time, statistical inference — you can ask questions about a whole population, not just about those who left writings.
- But the schema *is* an argument. The categories were designed by a state for a purpose — taxation, conscription, ideology. Imperial Russian censuses classifying people by language and confession, or Soviet statistics on "class", encode political decisions, not neutral facts.
- Missingness is systematic, not random: undercounted groups, destroyed record series, gaps during wars.
- The units are unstable: a "household", a "desyatina", a "ruble" do not mean the same thing across a century, so time series need historical calibration before they can be compared.

**The real difference is not bias vs objectivity — it is where the bias sits.** A memoir wears its subjectivity on the surface; a statistical table hides its subjectivity inside the schema and therefore looks objective. That makes structured data more dangerous if used uncritically.

**Data-science framing (your strong move here):** a historical source behaves like a measurement instrument with its own error model. Before analysis you have to reconstruct the data-generating process — who collected it, with what incentives, what was excluded — and treat survivorship bias as the default, not the exception.

## ВОПРОС 3
**How has the historical profession come into being, and what differentiates it from other branches of human knowledge?**

### What to say (EN)

**Before the profession.** In antiquity history was a branch of literature and rhetoric — Herodotus, Thucydides. In medieval Europe and Rus' it was written by monks as providential chronicles. In neither case was there a professional community with shared rules of verification.

**The birth of source criticism (17th–18th c.).** Scholars such as the Bollandists and Jean Mabillon (*De re diplomatica*, 1681) developed diplomatics and palaeography — systematic techniques to establish whether a charter was genuine. This is the technical foundation of the discipline. The Enlightenment added philosophical, sceptical narrative history (Voltaire, Gibbon).

**Professionalisation (19th c.).** The decisive moment. Several things happened together:
- Leopold von Ranke in Berlin insisted history be written from primary archival documents and introduced the *research seminar* as a training format.
- State archives were opened to researchers after the French Revolution and the Napoleonic era.
- Nation-states funded universities and wanted national histories, so history acquired institutional and political backing.
- Formal credentials appeared: the research doctorate; specialised journals (*Historische Zeitschrift* 1859, *English Historical Review* 1886, *American Historical Review* 1895); professional associations (American Historical Association, 1884); and handbooks of method (Langlois and Seignobos, 1898).

**Broadening (20th c.).** The Annales school (Bloch and Febvre, 1929) shifted attention from politics to structures, economies and mentalities; then came social and quantitative history, the cultural and linguistic turn, microhistory, global and gender history.

**What differentiates history from other branches of knowledge:**
- *Its object cannot be observed or repeated.* The past is gone. There is no experiment, no re-run, no direct measurement — only surviving traces.
- *Evidence is found, not generated.* A physicist designs the experiment that produces the data; a historian inherits whatever happened to survive and must work with a sample they did not choose.
- *It is idiographic more than nomothetic* — it explains particular configurations of events rather than deriving general laws (though it borrows models from economics, demography, sociology).
- *Explanation is often narrative* — showing how one situation turned into another — and interpretation of meaning is part of the task, not an obstacle to it.
- *But it is still empirical and disciplined*: claims must be grounded in sources, references must be checkable, and peer review makes them contestable. This is what separates history from memory, heritage, political myth and journalism, which also talk about the past but do not submit to source criticism.

**Banner's point:** history is not only a discipline but a set of *professions* — with training, credentials, ethical standards and many career paths: university researchers and teachers, archivists, museum curators, editors of documentary editions, public and government historians.

## ВОПРОС 4
**In what ways do digital tools and methods (databases, machine text analysis, digitized archives, GIS mapping, etc.) change how historians work with sources today compared to traditional methods?**

### What to say (EN)

**1. Scale.** Traditional research is bounded by what one person can read in a lifetime. Digitised corpora and text mining let historians work across millions of documents — Franco Moretti's "distant reading" as opposed to close reading. Topic modelling, named-entity recognition and word embeddings can trace how a concept or a vocabulary shifts across decades, which no individual reader could do by hand.

**2. Access and search.** Digitised archives and full-text search change the economics of research: you no longer need to travel to an archive and read a whole fond to find three relevant mentions. Handwritten text recognition (e.g. Transkribus) is now extending this to manuscripts, not just print.

**3. New objects of analysis.** Structured databases and prosopography make it possible to reconstruct careers and family networks at population scale. Network analysis maps correspondence and patronage (the Republic of Letters project). GIS and spatial history turn geography into an analytical variable rather than a backdrop — mapping trade routes, epidemics, deportations, urban change over time.

**4. Reproducibility and collaboration.** Code and datasets can be published alongside the argument, so another historian can rerun the analysis. This imports a norm from the sciences that traditional humanistic scholarship did not have.

**5. What is genuinely new vs what only looks new.** Quantitative history existed before computers (cliometrics in the 1960s–70s). What is new is scale, speed, and the ability to combine heterogeneous sources.

**Limitations, which matter as much as the gains:**
- *Digitisation bias*: only a fraction of archives is digitised, and the selection favours large, well-funded, mostly Western institutions and printed material. Searching only the digitised corpus silently reshapes the research question.
- *OCR and HTR errors* propagate into every downstream count; error rates are worse for older, non-Latin and handwritten material.
- *Decontextualisation*: a keyword search returns a sentence torn out of the document's structure and rhetoric. Frequency is not meaning.
- *False precision and black boxes*: a topic model produces clean-looking clusters whose interpretation is still an act of reading, and unexplainable models are hard to defend in a discipline that requires you to show your evidence.

**Conclusion:** digital methods do not replace source criticism, palaeography or archival work — they relocate them. The critical questions (who produced this, why, what is missing) now have to be asked of the *corpus* and of the *model*, not only of the individual document. The productive practice is hybrid — "scalable reading": use computation to find where to look, then read closely.

## ВОПРОС 5
**Present the main features of Medieval Russia's historical literature. How are chronicles different from the 'modern' way of writing history? Which historical concepts were developed at the Kievan and Moscow courts, and what role did the church play in this process?**

### What to say (EN)

**The dominant genre: the letopis' (chronicle).** Annalistic in form — entries organised year by year ("v leto..."), rather than by theme or argument. Composite: chronicles are *svody*, compilations in which each scribe copies earlier material and adds his own continuation, so a single manuscript layers several centuries of authorship. Largely anonymous or collective. Written and preserved in monasteries, because the church held the monopoly on literacy and on scriptoria.

**Key texts:**
- *The Tale of Bygone Years* (Primary Chronicle), compiled in the early 12th century at the Kiev Caves Monastery, traditionally attributed to the monk Nestor — though the extent of his authorship is debated. It narrates the origins of Rus', the calling of Rurik, Olga's revenge, and Vladimir's conversion in 988, framing Rus' history inside biblical and Byzantine history and legitimising the Rurikid dynasty.
- After the fragmentation of Kievan Rus' from the 1130s, regional chronicle traditions diverged: the *Laurentian Codex* (northeastern, supporting Vladimir-Suzdal and then Moscow), the *Hypatian Codex* (southern, including the Galician-Volhynian Chronicle, later foundational for Ukrainian historical identity), and the *Novgorod chronicles*, which record a republican rather than princely political order.

**Concepts developed at the Kievan court:** dynastic legitimacy through the Rurikid line; the unity of the Slavic land under one ruling house; Rus' as a new member of Christendom after the baptism of 988; history as the working-out of divine providence.

**Concepts developed at the Moscow court** (15th–16th c.), all serving centralisation and autocracy:
- *Translatio imperii*: "The Tale of the Princes of Vladimir" (early 16th c.) extended Rurikid genealogy back to Caesar Augustus, giving Moscow a Roman imperial pedigree.
- *Moscow as the Third Rome*: after the fall of Constantinople (1453) and Ivan III's marriage to Sophia Palaiologina (1472), the monk Philotheus of Pskov formulated Moscow's claim to be the last seat of true Christianity. "The Legend of the White Cowl" symbolised the passage of Christian authority from Rome through Constantinople to Moscow.
- Under Ivan IV, Metropolitan Macarius produced the *Book of Degrees* (Stepennaya kniga), which arranged Russian history as a genealogical ladder of saintly rulers — hagiography as state history — and the vast *Illustrated Chronicle* (Litsevoy letopisny svod) compiled Russian and world history in one official narrative. Both downplayed the role of the boyars in favour of the autocrat.
- The ideological work paid off institutionally: the Russian Church obtained autocephaly and the Moscow Patriarchate was established in 1589.

**The church's role**, summed up: it was the *producer* (monastic scriptoria), the *interpreter* (providential explanation — the Mongol invasion read as divine punishment for the sins of the princes), and the *co-author of state ideology* (Macarius, Philotheus), supplying the sacred vocabulary in which Muscovite autocracy described itself.

**How chronicles differ from modern history writing:**
| Chronicle | Modern history |
|---|---|
| Compilation and faithful copying are virtues | Originality and an explicit argument are required |
| Anonymous / collective | Named author, personally accountable |
| Divine providence as the cause of events | Social, economic, political causation |
| Fact, legend and hagiography mixed without distinction | Explicit source criticism, fact separated from tradition |
| No apparatus — no footnotes, no citation of evidence | Verifiable references; the reader can check the sources |
| Purpose: to legitimise a dynasty and edify the reader | Purpose: to explain; conclusions open to falsification |
| Sacred, eschatological time | Secular chronology, periodisation as an analytical tool |
| Audience: ruler and church | Audience: a professional community of peers |

**The point to end on:** we do not read chronicles as neutral reports of what happened — we read them as primary sources for the political and religious thinking of the people who compiled them. Their distortions are not a defect to be corrected; they are the evidence.
